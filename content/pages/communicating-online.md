::: intro
In a fully online course, what you write is all anyone sees of you. This page
says how to write it well, and it names the one thing you must never post.
:::

::: section | Where each kind of message goes
Pick the right place and you get a faster answer.

1. **Q and A Discussion Board.** Anything about the course, the labs, or an
   error. Public, and checked every weekday.
2. **Canvas Inbox.** Anything private: a grade, a deadline, a personal
   situation. Your ACES student email also works.
3. **Graded discussions.** Only the topic that discussion asks about.

Do not send course questions by text or from a personal email account. Those
may never arrive.
:::

::: section | The basics
You are held to the same standard as a student in a classroom. In this course
your writing is the only impression anyone gets, so it counts for more.

- **Skip the abbreviations.** Not everyone knows them, and this is a
  professional setting.
- **Do not type in all capitals.** It reads as shouting.
- **Go easy on exclamation points.** One is plenty.
- **Proofread before you post.** Spelling and punctuation prevent
  misunderstandings.
- **Think before you send.** You cannot take a post back.
- **Be patient with mistakes.** Everyone makes them, including your instructor.
:::

::: section | Tone is harder online than you think
Nobody can see your face. A short reply that felt efficient to you can read as
angry to someone else. Sarcasm almost never survives the trip.

Three habits fix most of it:

1. Say the helpful thing first, then the correction.
2. Ask a question instead of stating that someone is wrong.
3. Reread your post as though someone you respect wrote it to you.

Your classmates come from different ages, backgrounds, and first languages.
Assume good intent, and write so that good intent is easy to assume.
:::

::: section | Be professional, because this field is small
This is a security course, and the tone follows the ethics.

Write the way a practitioner writes. Say what you tested, what happened, and
what you concluded. Do not brag about breaking something. Do not describe an
attack on anything outside your own lab, even as a joke.

There is no edgy framing in this course and no glory in breaking things.
Breaking the lab target is the assignment, and doing it carefully is the skill.

One useful test before you post: would you be comfortable if a hiring manager
read this thread? Some of them will. People in this field read each other's
public writing, and your work here is meant to become a portfolio.
:::

::: callout stop | Never post a credential
Your API key is a credential. So is a password and so is a token.

1. Never paste a key into a discussion post, a message, or a chat.
2. Never post a screenshot with a key visible in a terminal, an editor, or a
   settings file. Crop it, or retype the part you need.
3. Never paste a key into a model prompt.

If a key does end up somewhere public, act the same minute. Turn it off at the
provider, make a new one, and tell your instructor. Nobody is in trouble for
reporting a leak quickly. Staying quiet is the part that causes harm.
:::

::: section | Privacy is a legal matter, not just a courtesy
FERPA is the federal law that protects student records. It applies to this
class.

So there are three things you do not do:

1. Do not share a classmate's post, work, or personal details with anyone
   outside this course.
2. Do not share your own private details with the class. Your home address,
   personal email, and login information stay off the board.
3. Do not share course materials, Zoom links, or lab data outside the course.

Everything in the lab is invented, and it stays that way. Never bring a real
ticket, a real customer, or anything from your job into a post.
:::

::: section | Disagreeing well
You will disagree about technical things. That is good, and it is how the field
works.

Disagree about the claim, not the person. Bring evidence: a result line, a file,
a line number. In this course an argument is settled by what the system did, not
by who sounded more certain.

If a thread gets heated, stop replying and message your instructor. Do not
answer in kind.
:::

::: section | Zoom, for office hours
Office hours are held over Zoom by appointment. A few rules make them work.

1. Sign in with your first and last name, so your instructor knows who is
   waiting.
2. Check what is behind you and what can be heard. Your camera may be asked
   for.
3. Mute yourself when you are not speaking.
4. Never share a meeting link or password with anyone outside the course.
5. Never disrupt a meeting or help someone else disrupt one. That carries
   consequences from the college.

Come with your exact error text and what you have already tried. Fifteen
focused minutes usually beats a week of guessing.
:::
