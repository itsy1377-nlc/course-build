::: intro
This page says what academic integrity means at Alamo Colleges, and what it
means in a course where everyone attacks the same target. Read it once now, and
again before your first submission.
:::

::: section | The rule, in one paragraph
The Alamo Colleges District has a procedure on academic integrity, numbered
F.4.2.2. The Student Code of Conduct applies to every course, online and in
person, with no difference.

The rule is simple. The work you turn in has to be your own. When you use
someone else's work, you say so.

Most of your work here is done off campus, with nobody watching. That is exactly
why this matters.
:::

::: section | The seven kinds of violation
The Student Code of Conduct names these. Each one is defined in plain language.

1. **Cheating.** Using materials or help you were not allowed to use. Copying
   someone's answers counts. So does letting someone copy yours.
2. **Plagiarism.** Using another author's words or ideas as if they were your
   own. It can happen by accident, and it still counts.
3. **Collusion.** Helping someone else be dishonest. Doing an assignment for a
   classmate is collusion. So is paying someone to do yours.
4. **Fabrication.** Making up information. Inventing a test result, changing a
   number, or citing a source you never read.
5. **Academic misconduct.** Breaking college rules on purpose. Changing a
   grade, pretending to be another student, or passing around exam questions.
6. **Academic enticement.** Offering an employee anything of value to change a
   grade, extend a deadline, or waive a requirement.
7. **Sale of academic product.** Selling or buying coursework. Tutoring and
   editing are fine. Writing someone's assignment for money is not.
:::

::: callout warning | Fabrication is the one to watch in this course
Every other course warns you about plagiarism. This one warns you about
**fabrication**, because security work is full of chances to invent a result.

These are all fabrication, and all are graded as violations:

1. Reporting an attack you did not run.
2. Editing a result line before you paste it into a report.
3. Writing that a fix held when you never re-ran the test.
4. Citing a framework identifier you did not look up.

An honest negative result is worth full credit. "I ran it five times and it
never worked" is a finding. A made-up success is a violation.
:::

::: section | What you may share with classmates
You are encouraged to talk to each other. Security is learned in the open, and
the Q and A board exists for that.

**You may share:**

- How an attack works, and why.
- What an error message means.
- Where to look in the code.
- A payload, meaning the text of an attack, discussed on the board.

**You may not share:**

- Your code for a graded module.
- Your finished report, or any part of it.
- Your run results, your evidence, or your quiz answers.
- Your API key, with anyone, for any reason.

Your lab holds fake secrets built from your own student ID. Those are called
canary tokens, and yours are different from everyone else's. A classmate can
hand you an idea. They cannot hand you your proof.

If a comprehension question asks for a value from your own run, the answer is
in your files. Copying a classmate's answer is cheating, and it is also wrong,
because their number is not your number.
:::

::: section | Citing your sources
Cite anything you did not write. That includes a framework document, a page
from this course, a blog post, and a snippet of someone's code.

A citation in this course does not need a formal style. It needs three things:
what the source is, where you found it, and which part you used.

Your college librarians will help with citation in any style your other courses
require. The Academic and Student Support page says how to reach them.
:::

::: section | What happens after a violation
Your instructor decides based on what happened.

1. The work receives a **zero**.
2. The violation is documented with Northeast Lakeview College.
3. Depending on how serious it was, you can receive an F for the course.
4. A violation of the Rules of Engagement is also referred to the Dean of
   Student Success, and it can carry consequences outside the college.

None of this is here to frighten you. It is here so nobody can say they did not
know.

If you are unsure whether something is allowed, ask first. Asking is never a
violation, and no one has ever been penalized for asking.
:::

::: callout note | AI has its own page
The district treats unauthorized AI use as an academic integrity violation.
Assignments may authorize it in writing, and in this course many of them do.

**This course permits AI assistance on all coding.** That permission comes with
conditions, and breaking those conditions is a violation.

Read the AI Use Policy page before you use any AI tool on course work. It is
the next page in this module.
