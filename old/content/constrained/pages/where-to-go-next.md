::: intro
Seven weeks is a start, not a finish. This page is an honest map of what to
learn next and why. It is ordered, and the first item is the one that pays for
all the others.
:::

::: section | Learn to program. That is the highest-value next step
This course had no programming prerequisite and it did not add one. It did put
you in the position of needing the skill, three times.

1. **Week 1.** You listed every tool your agent could reach and marked what
   could not be undone. You could only mark a tool once you understood what it
   touched.
2. **Week 5.** An assistant handed you a timeline. Catching the claim that did
   not hold was exactly your ability to read the record it named.
3. **Week 6.** You wrote a control yourself, inside one function, and then
   measured whether it held.

**You cannot supervise what you cannot read.** That is the closing argument of
this course, and you have three pieces of your own evidence for it.
:::

::: section | Why this matters more with a coding agent, not less
A coding agent writes code faster than you can read it. It also asks you to
approve what it wrote.

An approval step that cannot read what it is approving is not an approval step.
It is a button. The person clicking it still owns the result.

Reading well is also the cheapest defense you have. Reach is a property of how
one agent was set up, not of coding agents in general. Some are read-only. Some
ask before every command. Some can reach your whole disk. You find out by
reading the configuration, which is the same skill again.
:::

::: section | What "learn to program" means here
Not a language on a resume. Four things, in this order.

| Skill | What it looks like when you have it |
|---|---|
| Reading code | You open a short script and say what it reads, writes, and sends |
| Writing small programs | You write forty lines that do one job, and they run |
| Testing | You write a check that fails when the code is wrong |
| Version control | You can say what changed, when, and why |

Start with Python. The labs are written in it, it is already on your computer,
and it is what most security tooling is written in.

**The college already offers programming courses.** Ask your advisor which one
fits your degree plan and when to take it. Advising is listed on the Support
Resources page in Start Here, and its link is in the Canvas sidebar.
:::

::: section | The rest of the map
Each of these makes the work you did this term easier. None of them replaces the
first one.

| What to learn | Why it pays, in this course's terms |
|---|---|
| Reading logs at scale | Week 4 gave you one audit log. Real work gives you millions of rows |
| Identity and authorization | Week 4's finding was that nobody asked who was calling. That question is most of security |
| Operating systems and networks | You have to know what a process, a port, and a file permission are |

:::

::: section | Three documents worth reading properly
This course covered selected OWASP risks, not a whole framework. Three
publications are worth your own time:

1. The OWASP LLM Top 10 2026.
2. The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026.
3. MITRE ATLAS, release 2026.07.

Your instructor can point you at all three. The library can help you find any
professional document, and its link is in the Canvas sidebar.

Read them the way you read a record. Pick one entry you already met this term,
read what it says, and ask what evidence would show it in a system in front of
you.
:::

::: section | What this course did not cover
Seven weeks cannot hold the field. These are real areas, and none of them was
taught here:

- How models are built and trained.
- Several agents working together, and what goes wrong between them.
- What an agent remembers between sessions, and who else can read it.
- Tools written by other people, and the risk that comes with them.
- Running agents in production: scale, cost, monitoring, and change control.
- The legal and privacy side, which is a career of its own.
- Incident response as a process, beyond the single handover in week 5.

Knowing the edge of what you covered is a professional habit. Say it in
interviews. It reads as honesty, and it is.
:::

::: section | Keep practicing, and keep the scope
The Rules of Engagement do not expire when the course ends. Your permitted
targets stay the same: a practice target you own, an agent you built, and a
model on your own computer.

Build your own small lab. Break it. Nothing about the skill requires anyone
else's system, and the day you test one without permission is the day the skill
stops being an asset.

If you find a real weakness somewhere, the answer does not change either. Stop,
write down what you saw, and report it through the right channel. Ask your
instructor, or the security contact where you work.
:::

::: section | Your evidence file, after the course
It is the strongest thing most entry-level portfolios do not have: an assessment
of a live agentic system where every claim points at a record.

Showing it is optional and worth no points. If you do show it, two things first.
Your canary tokens are built from your college ID, so scrub them out. Then ask
your instructor before you publish anything from the labs, because the licence
question is not yours to answer alone.
:::

::: callout note | If you take one thing from this page
The habit is worth more than the subject. You now check a claim against the
thing that recorded it, and you say plainly what your evidence does not settle.

That habit is rare, it transfers to every part of this work, and no tool
replaces it.
:::
