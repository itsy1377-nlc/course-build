::: intro
This course teaches you to attack an AI agent. That skill is lawful on systems
you own and unlawful on systems you do not. This page draws that line. Read all
seven clauses. You will be asked to cite them.
:::

::: section | Clause 1. What you may attack
You may work with three things this term:

1. **The practice target** this course gives you. It runs on your own computer.
2. **The agent you build** in this course.
3. **A language model running on your own computer.**

That list is complete. If something is not on it, you may not attack it.
:::

::: section | Clause 2. What you may never attack
You may never attack:

- A commercial AI service. This includes every public chatbot and every hosted
  model API, whether free or paid, whether you have an account or not.
- Any computer, network, account, or service you do not own.
- Your college's systems, including Canvas.
- Another student's computer or accounts.

There is no version of this course where any of those is allowed. Not for a
better grade. Not to check something. Not because it seemed harmless.
:::

::: callout stop | The one that catches people
Testing a prompt injection on a public chatbot feels like nothing. It is not
nothing. It is unauthorized testing of somebody else's system, it breaks their
terms of use, and depending on what you do it can break state and federal law.
Use the practice target. It was built so you never need anything else.
:::

::: section | Clause 3. Stay inside the practice target
The practice target holds invented customers, invented tickets, and invented
records. None of it is real. Keep it that way.

- Do not put real personal data into it. Not yours, not anyone's.
- Do not put real passwords, card numbers, or account numbers into it.
- Do not connect it to any real service.
- Do not change it so it reaches the internet.
:::

::: section | Clause 4. If you find something real
You may notice a real weakness somewhere: in college software, in a product you
use, in something a family member owns. That is a good sign about you. It is
also the moment to stop.

1. Stop. Do not test it, not even once, not even gently.
2. Write down what you saw.
3. Tell your instructor.

Your instructor will tell you who to report it to. Reporting through the right
channel protects you. Testing first does not, even when your intent is good.
:::

::: section | Clause 5. What you do with what you learn
Everything here is taught so you can defend systems. Using it against a system
you do not own is not a grey area, and a course grade is not a defence.

Do not share working attacks outside this course. Do not post them publicly.
Discussing what you learned is fine and expected; handing someone a working
attack aimed at a live system is not.
:::

::: section | Clause 6. Your own machine, your own limits
Everything in this course runs on your computer. Nothing you do should reach
another machine.

- Run the practice target on your own computer only.
- Do not run it on college equipment or a shared server.
- Do not host it where other people can reach it.

If you cannot run something on your own machine, say so on the Questions and
Answers board. There is a supported path for every part of this course.
:::

::: section | Clause 7. What happens if you break this
Breaking clause 1 or clause 2 is an academic integrity violation. It is handled
under the district's academic integrity policy, which is linked on the Academic
Integrity page. Depending on what happened, it may also be reported outside the
college.

Being unsure is not a violation. Ask first, on the Questions and Answers board
or by message. Nobody has ever been penalised here for asking whether something
was in scope.
:::

::: callout note | Why this page exists
Every security course has a page like this, and most students skim it. This one
is short on purpose so you can actually read it. The three permitted targets in
clause 1 are the whole rule. If you remember one thing, remember the list.
:::
